Ba Vũng Archaeological Site

Khu phố Hạ Long 1, Đặc khu Vân Đồn – Archaeological
Photo of the surrounding area · Sở VHTT&DL Quảng Ninh (Cổng TTĐT tỉnh Quảng Ninh)

A type site of the Hạ Long culture on Cái Bầu island, both a settlement and a stone-tool workshop of prehistoric people some 4,500–3,000 years ago. Listed as a national monument in 2025.

Discovery and research

Ba Vũng lies on Cái Bầu, the largest island in Bái Tử Long Bay, in what was formerly Hạ Long commune (now Hạ Long 1 quarter, Vân Đồn Special Zone). Research here spans almost a century. In 1928 the French archaeologist M. Colani surveyed the area, which she called “Hayart”, and collected 70 flaked stone tools now held at the National Museum of Vietnamese History. In 1999, while re-surveying archaeological sites found in Quảng Ninh before 1954, researcher Hà Hữu Nga of the Institute of Archaeology and the Quảng Ninh Museum rediscovered the site. Three excavations followed, in 2001, 2003 and 2005, along with further surveys.

According to Assoc. Prof. Trình Năng Chung, the site originally covered about 105,000 m², but sand mining has reduced it to just over 30,000 m². Even so, it is considered the most intact Hạ Long culture site known to date and the largest in extent among the prehistoric coastal sites of the north-east.

According to Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung, writing in the 2025 Scientific Bulletin of the National Museum of Vietnamese History, the three excavations were modest in size: in October 2001 the Institute of Archaeology opened 101 m² (three main and three auxiliary trenches) with the participation of researcher Chen Wei Chun (Taiwan); in 2003 a 24 m² trench was opened jointly with the Quảng Ninh Museum; and in 2005 an 18 m² trench was laid out on a north–south axis, with the provincial Department of Culture and Information also taking part. The 2005 season dug in thin spits of about 10 cm, and all soil was dry-sieved and wet-screened.

The same source describes the 2005 stratigraphy: a grey sandy topsoil about 40 cm thick, a cultural layer averaging 50 cm and densely packed with stone tools, pottery and flakes, and sterile white sand from about 90 cm down – beach deposits from the Middle Holocene marine transgression. At a depth of 80–90 cm, archaeologists found for the first time 12 dark-soil pits (three large ones of almost 1 m², two of about 0.5 m² and seven small ones 20–30 cm across) containing pebbles, potsherds and stone fragments but no human remains; their function is still unclear, possibly burials or post holes.

Landscape and name

According to Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung, the name “Ba Vũng” (“Three Coves”) comes from three small sea coves – Vũng Tây, Vũng Giữa and Vũng Đông – to the south-east of Cái Bầu Island. The site is a roughly turtle-shell-shaped sand dune more than 160 m long on a north–south axis, linking Đá Trắng islet to the north with Hòn Lớn to the south; it lies within a sheltered cove facing Bái Tử Long Bay, more than 1 km from Cái Bầu Island. The dune backs onto limestone hills and faces the sea on one side, while behind it lies a mangrove valley fed by a freshwater stream – an environment well suited to settlement and to exploiting both sea and forest.

Dating and finds

Ba Vũng belongs to the Late Neolithic – Early Metal Age. Published dates vary slightly: the Quảng Ninh Department of Culture, Sports and Tourism gives about 4,500–3,000 years ago; the provincial heritage inventory gives 4,000–3,500 years; and Trình Năng Chung, using calibrated radiocarbon samples (4,820–4,450 BP), places it at about 4,500–3,500 years ago.

The volume of finds is remarkable: 109,749 stone artefacts, including a collection of pressure-flaked drill bits numbering 377 finished bits and 893 blanks; some 220,000 potsherds and one intact pottery bowl, now displayed at the Quảng Ninh Museum; as well as semi-precious stones and hundreds of thousands of flakes and core fragments. Single-shouldered axes and raw materials such as felsite and chert point to contacts with Guangxi and Guangdong in present-day China.

According to Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung, the site's main date comes from charcoal sample BV003, analysed by AMS at the Beta laboratory (USA) in 2001, which returned 4,130 ± 40 BP; two samples taken near the surface (150 ± 40 and 180 ± 40 BP) came from a disturbed, near-modern layer and are not used to date the site.

The same study shows that flakes and blades make up almost 80% of the stone assemblage, mostly of sandstone, followed by raw material, grinding stones and cores; chipped tools number only a few dozen, far fewer than in the earlier Soi Nhụ and Cái Bèo cultures. Polished tools include quadrangular adzes, harpoon points, stone knives, arrowheads, saws, hoes and chisels. Grooved grinding stones – known to researchers as the “Hạ Long mark” – were used to sharpen harpoons and points. The drill bits, about 1.2–2.2 cm long, were struck from blades and retouched by pressure flaking, mostly in felsite with a little opal and chert. Sandstone was quarried locally (at Hòn Nhỏ, south of the site), whereas felsite and chert were brought from further away. More than 2,000 lumps of red ochre were also found, possibly used as a pottery slip.

As for pottery, the study notes that sherds from 2001 were tempered with crushed shell and come in four main colours (light pink, yellowish red, brown and chalky grey); only a small share is decorated, mainly with cord marks, plus perforations, wave patterns, S-shaped motifs and applied relief. Pottery from 2005 is mostly coarse and porous, about 80% coated with a reddish-brown ochre slip; forms include cooking pots, jars, bowls, plates, vases and low-footed bowls. Among the objects presented by the Quảng Ninh Provincial Museum is a stone axe with sloping shoulders and no tang notch.

Life and economy of the inhabitants

According to Quảng Ninh Newspaper (2010), the people of Ba Vũng lived mainly by hunting and gathering plants. Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung adds that they exploited both marine and forest resources; the site has yielded some fish bones and clam and scallop shells, along with harpoon points probably used to collect shellfish. There is no direct evidence of farming yet, although the inhabitants may have grown tubers at the foot of the hills near the stream.

The most striking feature is production for exchange: according to the author, Ba Vũng made stone tools, above all drill bits, to trade with ornament-making centres such as Tràng Kênh and Bãi Tự. Its drills and drill blanks resemble those from Bãi Bến (Cát Bà); its raw materials and cores are similar to the Xiqiaoshan (Tây Tiều Sơn) site group in Nanhai, Guangdong; and its grooved grinding stones and porous pottery also appear at Hoa Lộc (Thanh Hóa).

Significance

Ba Vũng was both an open-air settlement and a stone-tool workshop, especially for adzes and drill bits, and is regarded as the clearest example of such a workshop on the north-eastern coast. The scale of production suggests a fairly developed society with specialised stone-working and networks for exchanging goods with other groups.

Researchers see Ba Vũng as the starting point of the Hạ Long culture in Vân Đồn. After this stage, ancient Hạ Long communities at places such as Ngọc Vừng and Đông Trong gradually adapted to and exploited the marine environment. Together with Soi Nhụ, Hà Giắt and Đông Trong, Ba Vũng helps establish Vân Đồn as one of the cradles of major prehistoric cultures in the north-east; according to the Institute of Archaeology, more than ten prehistoric sites have been found in Vân Đồn.

Listing

The site was listed as a provincial monument by Decision No. 3923/QĐ-UBND of 13 December 2011 of the Quảng Ninh People's Committee; on 14 April 2025 the Minister of Culture, Sports and Tourism signed Decision No. 1011/QĐ-BVHTTDL designating it a national monument. Protection zone I covers 5,980 m² and zone II 24,854 m². Listing provides a legal basis for managing, conserving and promoting the site in the face of development pressure.

Conservation and challenges

According to Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung, the site has long suffered from sand mining and encroachment, and urbanisation together with resort and sports projects nearby continues to put pressure on it. The author argues that protecting and promoting Ba Vũng requires persistent, long-term effort oriented towards a heritage economy; he also mentions the concept of “community archaeology” proposed by researcher Hà Hữu Nga, who took Ba Vũng itself as his case study.

In the overview of Vân Đồn Special Zone published on the Quảng Ninh Provincial Portal (14 January 2026), Ba Vũng is listed as one of the zone's three national-level relics.

Visitor guide

The site is on Cái Bầu island and can be reached by road from central Cái Rồng or from the Hạ Long – Vân Đồn Expressway. It is an open-air archaeological site with no on-site visitor facilities or displays; key finds are shown at the Quảng Ninh Museum. Visitors wishing to see the site should contact the Special Zone's Culture and Society Office in advance.

Please do not dig or collect potsherds or stone fragments in the site area.
