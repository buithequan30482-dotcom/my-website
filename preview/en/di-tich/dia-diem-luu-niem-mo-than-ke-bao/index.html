Kế Bào Coal Mine Memorial Site

Thôn Vạn Yên 2, Đặc khu Vân Đồn – Historical
Báo Quảng Ninh (bài: Huỳnh Đăng)

Remains of the Kế Bào coal mine, founded by the French on 1 September 1888 and regarded as Vietnam's first coal mine; iron bridges, mountain tunnels, a ventilation shaft and a coal chute at Vạn Hoa port survive.

History

The Kế Bào coal mine lies on the eastern side of the Cái Bầu peninsula in the former Vạn Yên commune and covers about 14 km². On 28 April 1888 the French Tonkin Coal Mining Company (S.F.C.T.) was founded, and on 1 September 1888 it established the Kế Bào mine – regarded as Vietnam's first coal mine. On 9 December 1911 the Kế Bào Coal Company was created with an initial capital of 30 million francs. At its peak the mine employed nearly 6,000 workers and produced more than 30,000 tonnes; coal passed through the screening plant in Vạn Yên and was shipped out from Vạn Hoa port.

According to Báo Quảng Ninh ("Vietnam's first coal mine", 10/01/2016), Vietnamese people had mined coal in Đông Triều from around 1840, but only by hand and on a small scale; large-scale mining began only after the French conquest of Tonkin. The article says the Governor-General of Indochina granted the whole mining area to the arms dealer Jean Dupuis (transcribed in the article as "Giăng-đuy-puy") without any contract with the Nguyễn court, as a reward for helping France take Tonkin. The French built a road of more than 10 km linking the mine to Vạn Hoa port, cutting through hills and building iron and concrete bridges. The article also reports that in 1890 the mine produced 30,242 tonnes of coal with 2,750 workers; between 1888 and 1935 French capital opened as many as 80 coal mines in the Quảng Ninh area, and Kế Bào is regarded as the birthplace of Vietnam's mining workforce.

Kế Bào and Port-Wallut in French records

According to research by Dr. Trần Xuân Thanh and Assoc. Prof. Dr. Nguyễn Mạnh Dũng (Social Sciences Information Journal, No. 3/2023), the Kế Bào mine belonged to the Société Française des Charbonnages du Tonkin (SFCT, initial capital 4 million francs); Governor-General De Lanessan (in office 1891–1894) personally attended the inauguration of one of the first coal shafts at Kế Bào, which was named after him, and in 1893 he visited the anchorage of Port-Wallut – today Vạn Hoa port in Vạn Yên commune. The French once hoped Port-Wallut would surpass Hải Phòng, but in practice it remained a secondary transshipment hub behind Hòn Gai and Cẩm Phả.

Contemporary French press articles compiled by the archive site entreprises-coloniales.fr record that a metre-gauge railway about 12–13 km long linked Port-Wallut with the mining area; in 1901 the Société civile du Domaine de Kébao was formed and in 1911 it became a joint-stock company; Kế Bào coal was shipped to Shanghai and Hong Kong. In the 1920s output rose from about 13,000 tonnes (1921) to about 60,500 tonnes (1929), and the workforce was almost entirely Chinese. According to L'Éveil économique (1927, 1929), Port-Wallut had a quay about 61 m long, cranes, storage for 20,000–30,000 tonnes of coal and, by 1929, could receive ships of up to 8,000 tonnes.

Decline and struggle

The world economic crisis of 1929–1933 brought mining to a standstill; by 1935 only about 600 workers remained. In 1929, 26 Kế Bào workers rose up, the first of the struggles that led to the general strike of 1936. When the mine area was liberated in 1955, the French dismantled and removed all its infrastructure and machinery.

Báo Quảng Ninh (10/01/2016) gives more detail on the 1929 uprising: 26 workers pushed two French soldiers down a mine shaft and then fled. The struggles that followed culminated in the general strike of some 30,000 miners on 12/11/1936. According to Báo Quảng Ninh (28/6/2020), by 1935 the mine had only 600 Asian workers and 2 Europeans left.

According to Báo Quảng Ninh ("1936 – The flame from the mining region", 25 Sep 2026), during the miners' general strike wave of 1936, workers at the Ke Bao coal mine, together with the Cai Da and Dong Dang mines, went on strike simultaneously on 24 and 25 November 1936.

What remains

The heritage list records that the French built nearly 10 mine galleries, 14 km of tramway and 2 tunnels through the hills, among other works. Many structures are now ruins (the screening house, iron bridges, ventilation shaft entrances, the Vạn Hoa port supervisor's house), while some, such as the tunnels and blockhouses, remain in good condition. Quảng Ninh Newspaper notes three surviving iron bridges on the coal line to Vạn Hoa port, two tunnels through the hills about 100 m long and about 4.5 m high and wide on a public road, and the old coal chute at Vạn Hoa port.

According to Báo Quảng Ninh (10/01/2016), the mine site still has house foundations, traces of the tramway, a mine entrance, a winch base, several blockhouses and a ventilation shaft; one bridge on the road to the port is known locally as "cầu Min-nơ" or "cầu Cái Làng". The 2020 article describes the iron bridges as rusted at the bolts and railings but structurally intact; the two tunnels through the hill (cống chui) are covered in moss, with dripping water forming stalactites; at Vạn Hoa port only the coal chute where ships once loaded coal remains.

Conservation

The site was inventoried in 2020 with a protected area of 6,670 m² (mine, tunnels, iron bridges) and has been proposed for provincial-level classification (expected by 2030).

Báo Quảng Ninh (10/01/2016) reported that the Vietnam National Coal–Mineral Industries Group (TKV) and the provincial Folk Arts Association had planned to build a stele house marking the site on a plot of about 100 m² (a 36 m² stele house of faux-wood construction with a surrounding wall about 0.7 m high). Vạn Yên commune leaders then estimated the protection zone at about 3 ha, mostly forest and residential land already allocated to households, so coordination with residents would be needed. However, according to Báo Quảng Ninh (28/6/2020), this plan to demarcate, mark and preserve the site had not been carried out and the relic was deteriorating; the commune's leaders called for clear mechanisms and policies to preserve it in connection with tourism development.

Visitor guide

The site is on Cái Bầu Island, Vân Đồn's largest island, which is reached by road. From Hạ Long, take the Hạ Long – Vân Đồn expressway; Vân Đồn International Airport is also located in the special zone.

The two tunnels are on a public road in the former Vạn Yên commune and can be seen from the road. The iron bridges and mine entrances have deteriorated – do not climb on them or enter the galleries. The Vạn Hoa port area is managed by a naval unit – do not enter without permission.
