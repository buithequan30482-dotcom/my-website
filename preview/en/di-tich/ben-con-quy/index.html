Con Quy Landing

Thôn Minh Châu, Đặc khu Vân Đồn – Historical
Ảnh minh họa do hệ thống tạo – cần thay ảnh chính thức

An ancient landing identified by archaeologists as one of the two earliest centres of the Vân Đồn trading port (late 12th – early 13th century); today it is a ruin.

Place in the port system

The heritage list records Con Quy as an ancient landing of the Vân Đồn trading port from the Lý dynasty, in the former Minh Châu commune; it was inventoried in 1996 and covers about 2,000 m². Quảng Ninh Newspaper names Con Quy as one of the main sites of the port system.

According to Phạm Quang Tuấn, Dương Thị Thủy and Lê Phương Thúy in the VNU Journal of Science (2015), Quan Lạn island still preserves traces of several ancient boat landings: Cái Làng and Cống Cái in Quan Lạn commune, and Con Quy wharf in Minh Châu commune; the remains consist of Tang–Song Chinese ceramic sherds and bowls and Vietnamese coins of the Lý, Trần and Lê periods. When reporting on Decision 1225/QĐ-TTg (2023), Báo Văn Hóa (24 October 2023) and Sài Gòn Giải Phóng (25 October 2023) likewise grouped Con Quy with Cái Cổng as the Minh Châu wharves within the port's roughly 200 km² network.

Archaeological findings

According to research by Đinh Thị Thanh Nga (Journal of Archaeology, 2023), Con Quy and Cống Cái were the earliest centres of the port, in the late 12th – early 13th century. A 2003 test excavation opened 3 pits (14 m²) and recovered 91 ceramic sherds (66 Vietnamese, 25 Chinese) and 32 coins; the Chinese ceramics date to the 13th–14th centuries and the Vietnamese to the 14th–17th centuries. The cultural layer had been disturbed by sand mining.

Đinh Thị Thanh Nga's article (Journal of Archaeology, 2023) also summarises earlier surveys. In 1936 Yamamoto Tatsuto collected Tang and Song Chinese coins, Vietnamese coins of the Revival Lê and Nguyễn periods, and ceramics from the Song to the Qing, and judged this wharf on the north of the island to be the best one, sheltered from wind and waves. In 1970 Đỗ Văn Ninh found the ceramics at Con Quy similar to those at Cái Làng and Cống Cái but fewer, and suggested it may have been a gateway station where boats waited to complete “formalities” before entering the main wharf.

According to the same article, the 2003 test excavation was carried out by Vietnam National University, Hanoi, with Showa University (Đại học Chiêu Hòa, Japan); of the 32 coins recovered, one was the Vietnamese “Thiên Phúc Trấn Bảo” and 31 were Chinese, 27 of them from the Song dynasty. The author hypothesises that Con Quy may have been an early Han Chinese settlement and joined the trade network earlier than other sites on Quan Lạn island.

Current state

The site is a ruin with no surviving structures. Con Quy is not among the components of the Vân Đồn Trading Port Complex (VD01) classified as a Special National Monument, but it is an important source for understanding how the port began.

In terms of terrain, the Journal of Archaeology (2023) describes Con Quy as a low hill about 1.5 km north-east of Cống Cái cove; pottery and stoneware are most concentrated around the junction where the Mang river flows into Cống Cái.

Con Quy port project (2026)

According to Báo Đấu thầu (27 June and 6 July 2026), the Vân Đồn Special Zone construction investment project management board has selected a contractor for the project to build Con Quy port in (former) Quan Lạn commune, with a total investment of 175.48 billion VND; the construction and equipment package was awarded at 138.77 billion VND with a 510-day schedule. The new port is intended to replace the existing piers in Quan Lạn and Minh Châu, which have deteriorated, silted up and cannot receive boats at low tide. The articles do not mention the ancient wharf relic, and no information has been published on the new port's exact position relative to the inventoried site.

Visitor guide

The site's exact location is not yet in the project's data and there are no visitor facilities.

This is a ruin with no visitor facilities or on-site guiding. Please do not dig or collect artefacts (ceramic sherds, bricks, stones, etc.) – all artefacts at heritage sites are protected by law.
