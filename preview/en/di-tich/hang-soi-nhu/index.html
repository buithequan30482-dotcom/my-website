Soi Nhụ Cave

Đặc khu Vân Đồn – Archaeological
Cổng TTĐT Vịnh Hạ Long – Ban Quản lý Di sản thế giới vịnh Hạ Long – Yên Tử

A Stone Age cave site discovered in the early 1960s, whose name has been given to the "Soi Nhụ culture" – a prehistoric culture that preceded the Cái Bèo and Hạ Long cultures in the Hạ Long – Bái Tử Long bay area.

Discovery

Soi Nhụ Cave lies in the former Hạ Long commune of Vân Đồn; Quảng Ninh Newspaper places the site on Cái Bầu Island in Bái Tử Long Bay. It was discovered in the early 1960s. The heritage list describes it as a dwelling place of Stone Age people; it was inventoried in 1996 and covers about 500 m².

According to Báo Quảng Ninh (article "Bái Tử Long – A Land of Historical and Cultural Layers", 2013), Soi Nhụ Cave lies about 8 km from Cái Rồng Port and was excavated by Vietnamese archaeologists in 1967. Báo Nhân Dân, citing Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung, notes that the site sits on the shore of Cái Bầu, the largest island of Bái Tử Long Bay, facing the open sea.

The "Soi Nhụ culture"

Based on this and similar sites, the archaeologist Hà Hữu Nga proposed the concept of a "Soi Nhụ culture", although some researchers remain cautious about it. According to an article by Trình Năng Chung in the National Museum of Vietnamese History's 2025 scientific bulletin, the Soi Nhụ culture dates to roughly 18,000–7,000 years ago and preceded the Cái Bèo and Hạ Long cultures; the lower Soi Nhụ cave was also inhabited by people of the Hạ Long culture.

According to Báo Quảng Ninh, archaeologist Hà Hữu Nga reaffirmed the concept of the Soi Nhụ culture in his book Hạ Long in Prehistoric Times (Hạ Long thời tiền sử); research on material from Thủng Cave (Phất Cờ Island) is seen as partial support for this view. Báo Nhân Dân summarises other assessments: Assoc. Prof. Dr. Nguyễn Khắc Sử groups 12 Soi Nhụ sites on the limestone islands of Hạ Long and Bái Tử Long bays into an early and a late phase, spanning the end of the Pleistocene to the beginning of the Holocene; Prof. Dr. Lâm Thị Mỹ Dung places Soi Nhụ in the early to middle Neolithic; and Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung regards the Soi Nhụ site as a hub of cultural exchange between northern Vietnam, southern China and Southeast Asia, contemporary with the Hòa Bình – Bắc Sơn cultures but occupying a different living environment.

Life of the early inhabitants

The evidence shows that the Soi Nhụ people gathered freshwater snails, hunted and made limited use of pebble tools. Nhân Dân Newspaper counts some 26 sites in the Hạ Long Bay area belonging to the successive Soi Nhụ – Cái Bèo – Hạ Long cultures, spanning roughly 18,000 to 3,500 years ago.

Human Remains and Excavated Finds

According to Báo Nhân Dân, Prof. Dr. Lâm Thị Mỹ Dung states that human skeletal remains of the Soi Nhụ culture have so far been found only at Soi Nhụ Cave: skull fragments, teeth, mandibles and limb bones of at least five individuals (three women and two men), all over 30 years old. According to Báo Quảng Ninh (2014), the 1967 excavation recovered more than 400 fish vertebrae and countless shells of various kinds; because no net-making tools were found, researchers believe the inhabitants mainly gathered marine resources rather than fishing with nets.

In an article by Assoc. Prof. Dr. Trình Năng Chung in the 2025 Scientific Bulletin of the National Museum of Vietnamese History, marine transgressions directly affected the Soi Nhụ people, forcing some groups to move to the mountains or higher ground. Báo Nhân Dân adds that sea level rose after about 9,000 years ago and peaked around 6,500–5,000 years ago at 4–5 m above today's level, so only sites in elevated positions have survived.

Significance

Soi Nhụ Cave is a key link in understanding how people lived in and adapted to the limestone islands and sea of north-eastern Vietnam in prehistoric times.

Visitor guide

Soi Nhụ Cave is an archaeological site that is not open to visitors, and its precise coordinates are not yet in the project's data. Please do not enter the cave on your own, dig or collect artefacts – archaeological sites are protected by law.
