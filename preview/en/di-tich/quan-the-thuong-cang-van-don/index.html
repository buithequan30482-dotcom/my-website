Vân Đồn Trading Port Complex

Thôn Thắng Lợi, Thôn Quan Lạn 1, thôn Quan Lạn 2, thôn Quan Lạn 3, Đặc khu Vân Đồn – Historical
Cổng Thông tin điện tử tỉnh Quảng Ninh

Founded in 1149 under King Lý Anh Tông, Vân Đồn was a network of harbours spread across the islands of Bái Tử Long Bay and served for centuries as Đại Việt's gateway to international trade. In 2023 the complex was designated a Special National Monument.

History

According to the Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư (Complete Annals of Đại Việt), in 1149 merchant fleets from Java (Trảo Oa), Lộ Lạc and Siam (Xiêm La) asked to stay and trade, and King Lý Anh Tông established the Vân Đồn estate among the islands to manage this commerce. Historians therefore regard Vân Đồn as the first trading port of Đại Việt.

What sets Vân Đồn apart is that it was never a single harbour. It was a network of anchorages scattered across the islands of Bái Tử Long Bay, covering roughly 200 km², including Cống Đông, Cống Tây, Cái Làng, Cống Cái, Cái Cổng, Con Quy, Cống Yên and Cống Hẹp. Assoc. Prof. Đặng Văn Bài describes it as a coastal-and-island harbour system, quite unlike river ports such as Phố Hiến or Hội An, which served both as an international trading hub and as a naval base defending the north-east.

Under the Lý dynasty, foreign trade was a state monopoly: exports included agarwood, pearls, ivory, cinnamon, rhinoceros horn, gold, silver, copper and seafood, while silk brocade was the main import. The Trần period was the port's heyday, drawing ships from many countries. According to the Quảng Ninh provincial portal, Vân Đồn was raised to the status of a military district (trấn) in 1345 under King Trần Dụ Tông; some other sources give the year as 1349. These waters are also tied to the victory of 1288, when Trần forces under Prince Nhân Huệ Vương Trần Khánh Dư destroyed the supply fleet of the Yuan general Trương Văn Hổ, paving the way for the great victory at Bạch Đằng.

Under the early Lê dynasty, trade with foreign ships was strictly controlled by the Hồng Đức legal code; under the Mạc, foreign trade revived. From the late 17th century, as Kẻ Chợ (Thăng Long), Phố Hiến and Hội An opened to Asian and Western merchants, Vân Đồn gradually lost its central role, and by the early Nguyễn period (19th century) it had ceased to operate, its old anchorages becoming fishing landings and places of local exchange.

A study published in the Journal of Archaeology (No. 2/2023) shows that the port's centre shifted over time: in its formative phase (late 12th – early 13th century) it lay around Con Quy and Cống Cái; in its developed phase (mid-13th – 16th century) at Cái Làng and Thừa Cống island (Cống Đông – Cống Tây), peaking in the late 14th – 15th centuries; and in its decline (17th – 18th century) it moved back to Cống Cái. A monograph by Prof. Nguyễn Văn Kim argues that Vân Đồn operated continuously from the 11th to the late 18th century.

Successive dynasties kept a close eye on these waters. According to Nhân Dân newspaper, King Lý Anh Tông toured the north-eastern sea in 1171 and 1172 and ordered border maps drawn; the Trần court appointed coastal officials such as the Quan trấn, Quan lộ and Sát hải sứ, together with the Bình hải naval guard.

The Battle of Vân Đồn, 1288

The Vân Đồn sea is linked to a decisive battle of the third resistance against the Yuan. According to the Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư (as cited by Giáo dục Việt Nam), around the twelfth lunar month of the Đinh Hợi year, that is early 1288, a supply fleet of about 70 ships carrying 170,000 thạch of grain under Zhang Wenhu (Trương Văn Hổ) entered the Vân Đồn waters after its naval escort under Omar (Ô Mã Nhi) had gone ahead.

Trần forces led by Prince Nhân Huệ Trần Khánh Dư intercepted it; most of the grain ships were sunk or captured, and Zhang Wenhu escaped with a single ship to Qiongzhou. Deprived of supplies, the Yuan army was forced to consider withdrawal, paving the way for the great victory at Bạch Đằng in April 1288. On Quan Lạn island, the Trần Khánh Dư shrine and the annual Quan Lạn Communal House festival keep the memory of this victory alive.

The sites

The complex has two main clusters. On Thắng Lợi island are Coves 1, 2 and 3, the Stupa (Bảo tháp), Cây Quéo Pagoda, Lấm Pagoda and its cove, Cát Pagoda and its cove, Chuồng Bò Cove and Huyện Cove. The twin islands of Cống Đông and Cống Tây lie side by side, separated by a deep channel well suited to mooring and transhipment. Their coves are still thick with Vietnamese and Chinese ceramic sherds from the 13th to 17th centuries, and Cống Tây bears traces of five pagodas (Lấm, Cát, Vụng Chuồng Bò, Trong and Vụng Cây Quéo) and a Trần-dynasty tower. Many finds are now displayed at the Quảng Ninh Museum: a turtle-shaped stone stele base from Vụng Cây Quéo Pagoda, stone balustrades carved with dragons from Lấm Pagoda, and tower bricks inscribed with their storey positions. Under the Nguyễn, Cống Tây was the seat of Nghiêu Phong district, remembered in the local phrase “Cống Đông thập bát xã” (the eighteen communes of Cống Đông).

On Quan Lạn island are Cái Làng landing, identified by many researchers as the heart of the port; Quan Lạn Communal House and Pagoda and the shrine of the three Phạm brothers; the Trần Khánh Dư Shrine (Nghè Trần Khánh Dư); Chổi Well (Giếng Chổi); Lòng Dinh Shrine; and the Mang River. At Cái Làng stands Hệu Well, an ancient stone-lined freshwater well about 2 m deep, said to have been dug in the Lý period to supply residents and visiting ships.

Archaeological research here began early: according to Quảng Ninh newspaper, the Institute of Archaeology first excavated the pagoda ruins on Cống Tây island in 1971. In 2004 the heritage certificate for the Vân Đồn port was first issued for the Cống Đông area; after a petition from Quan Lạn residents, the Ministry issued a second certificate with the same content for the Cái Làng site.

Legends and beliefs

Hệu Well at Cái Làng is also known as Hợi Well, Golden Turtle Well, Fairy Well, Jade Well and Tears Well. According to Quảng Ninh newspaper it is nearly 900 years old; its water is clear and not salty despite being right by the sea, its level barely changes between rainy and dry seasons, and merchant ships once stopped here for fresh water before sailing on. Legend says that after the well was dug a golden turtle appeared in a rock crevice and was revered as the guardian of the spring; Quan Lạn people call the well “the village's eye”.

About 1 km from the well, on a mound locally called Đình Hill and under an old banyan tree, a small shrine honours King Lý Anh Tông, founder of the Vân Đồn trading post, as the village's tutelary deity. The shrine was built more than 300 years ago and keeps a small jackfruit-wood statue of similar age. Villagers say the original Quan Lạn Communal House stood on this hill and was moved to its present site after the port declined.

Festival

The traditional Quan Lạn Communal House festival is held every year in the middle of the sixth lunar month, with the main day on the 18th, and was inscribed on the National List of Intangible Cultural Heritage by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism on 20 December 2019. According to Pháp luật Việt Nam newspaper, it honours the village deity's part in the 1288 victory over the Yuan and the occasion on which the village received a royal title.

The rites include the bathing of the deity's statue, raising the festival flags, welcoming the deity and offerings at the communal house. The highlight is the rowing race on the afternoon of the 18th between the Đông Nam Văn and Đoài Bắc Võ teams over about 2,000 m, seen as a re-enactment of the sea battle at Vân Đồn.

Significance

The provincial heritage inventory describes the Vân Đồn port as one of the few seaports of feudal Đại Việt, marking the country's entry into the international maritime system; the religious sites in the complex are linked to the port's activity and to Trần Khánh Dư's victory on the Mang River. At a 2022 scientific conference with 34 papers, scholars described Vân Đồn as a large-scale international trading centre of strategic defensive importance, noting that the ceramic deposit at Cái Làng stretches some 200 m along the shore and remains relatively intact.

On 24 October 2023 the Prime Minister signed Decision No. 1225/QĐ-TTg designating the Vân Đồn Trading Port Complex a Special National Monument. Its protected zones total almost 978,300 m².

A scientific conference in Quảng Ninh on 24 September 2022 brought together more than 100 delegates and 34 papers, four of them by international experts. Professor Nguyễn Văn Kim described Vân Đồn as Đại Việt's first international trading port, with the longest continuous history of any Vietnamese trading port; Professor Vũ Minh Giang stressed its international character, with foreign merchants trading and living there. Dr Lê Thị Liên of the Vietnam Archaeological Association warned that many remains are deteriorating and need coordinated protection.

Current condition

Quan Lạn Communal House and Pagoda, the shrine of the Phạm brothers, the Trần Khánh Dư Shrine and Lòng Dinh Shrine have been restored; the remaining sites are mostly ruins. The province has set out a conservation master plan for 2023–2025 and component projects for 2026–2030.

The pagodas on Thắng Lợi island, such as Lấm, Trong, Vụng Chuồng Bò and Cát, have not been allocated public investment; according to Quảng Ninh newspaper (August 2022), they are being restored with community funding once the district has appraised and approved each plan.

Visitor guide

The Quan Lạn sites (Cái Làng landing, the communal house, pagoda, shrines) can be reached by passenger boat from Ao Tiên Port to Quan Lạn harbour, then by electric cart or motorbike on the island. The Thắng Lợi sites (Cống Đông – Cống Tây) are mostly ruins and reachable only by boat; please contact the special zone authorities or local residents before going. Boat schedules vary with season and weather, so check with the port for the latest timetable.

At the ruins, please do not dig or remove ancient ceramic sherds; at places of worship, dress modestly and keep quiet.
